Bridge the gap between your AI policy and git commit.

Most organizations have a folder of PDFs that say "this is our policy" and an engineering reality where those policies are unenforced. The gap

Compliance dies between the document and the commit.

The work of writing a policy and the work of enforcing it sit in different worlds, owned by different teams, expressed in different formats. The policy says "don't paste customer data into AI tools"; the developer's IDE has no idea that rule exists. PolicyForge closes that loop.

How it works

Three steps. About fifteen minutes.

No accounts, no telemetry. The CLI runs locally; the web app keeps everything in memory. Your policy never leaves your machine unless you choose to host it elsewhere.

01
Upload your policy

Markdown, plain text, or Word document. PolicyForge extracts the policy text and runs it against a transparent baseline of rules cited from named public frameworks.

A severity-rated report tells you which rules your policy satisfies, which it partially addresses, and which are missing entirely. Every finding cites a specific rule, every rule cites a specific framework.

Why trust this

A tool that reviews policies has to earn the right to do so.

The defensibility of PolicyForge isn't the code — it's the baseline the code measures against, and the governance that keeps it current.

— 01
Citations are mandatory.

Every rule in the baseline must cite a named, public framework. The loader rejects rules without citations — it's enforced in code, not convention. Vendor whitepapers and individual blog posts are explicitly not acceptable sources.

— 02
The baseline is auditable.

The full set of rules lives in one YAML file in the repository. You can read it, fork it, propose changes, or run a fork against your own policy. There's no proprietary scoring engine behind the curtain.

— 03
No accounts, no tracking.

The CLI runs locally; the web app holds policies in memory only and writes nothing to disk on the server. Self-host it on your own infrastructure if your organization requires that — Docker compose included.

— 04
Apache 2.0, no paid tier.

A tool that finds problems with your policy and then sells you the fix has a conflict of interest. PolicyForge avoids it by being free, end to end. The toolkit it generates is yours to modify and ship.

An honest accounting

What this tool isn't.

The category is too important to oversell. Here is what PolicyForge does not do, said plainly.

Not legal advice.

PolicyForge identifies gaps against published frameworks. It does not certify compliance. Pair its output with review by your Legal and Compliance teams. The tool is opinionated about technical enforcement — it doesn't have an opinion about your jurisdiction's regulatory scope.

Not a replacement.

A staff security engineer reading your policy will catch things this tool won't. Use it as a starting point, not an endpoint. The most useful workflow is: run PolicyForge first, then have a human review the findings and add their own.

Not a community yet.

v0.1 is a working prototype. The single-maintainer pattern for security tooling is a known anti-pattern — baselines go stale within a year without active stewardship. The project actively needs co-maintainers, and treating it as production-ready before that exists would be irresponsible.

Not exhaustive.

Sixty rules across three baselines cover the well-trodden ground in AI usage policy, secure coding standards, and incident response. They don't cover everything. The engine supports adding more — vendor risk management, data retention, application privacy — the YAML rules need to be authored. Contributions welcome.

Common questions

Things you'll probably want to know.

Does my policy get sent to a server somewhere?

No. The CLI is fully local. The hosted web app holds uploads in memory only and writes nothing to disk. If your organization needs stronger guarantees than a hosted service can provide, self-host with the included Docker compose file — it runs entirely inside your network.

Will the generated toolkit work for my stack?

The current build supports TypeScript, C#, Python, Java, and mixed-stack repositories, paired with either GitHub Actions or Azure DevOps. The pre-commit layer uses Husky for Node-based and mixed repos, and Husky.NET for .NET-only repos. If your stack isn't represented, the engine is policy-type agnostic — adding a new stack is a template addition, not a rewrite.

How is this different from a Word-template policy generator?

A template generator helps you write the policy. PolicyForge does the opposite — it takes a policy you already have and generates the technical enforcement around it. The two are complementary; PolicyForge is most useful once you have a policy in hand and need to operationalize it.

Who maintains the baseline?

Currently a single maintainer, which the project treats as a known weakness. The governance model is documented in BASELINE_METHODOLOGY.md — six-month review cadence, mandatory citations, dispute process. Recruiting co-maintainers is the most important non-code priority for this project right now.

Can I trust the findings against my real policy?

You should treat them the way you'd treat the output of a static analysis tool: directionally useful, occasionally wrong, always worth a human review. Every finding cites the specific rule that fired and the framework it traces back to, so you can defend or dispute each one. False positives can be filed as issues; the dispute process is in the methodology doc.

What does PolicyForge cost?

Nothing, ever. Apache 2.0, no paid tier, no managed service plans. A tool that finds problems with your policy and then sells you the fix has a conflict of interest the project explicitly avoids by staying free.
